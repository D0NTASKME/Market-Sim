What's happening

Ten uninformed traders buy and sell at random around the current price. One market maker quotes a price to buy just below it and a price to sell just above, twenty times a second, and pockets the difference. Against random flow that is a steady business, which is why the teal line climbs.

The informed trader sees the asset's true value, the dashed line, before the market does. The slider sets how often it looks. It only trades when the price is wrong, and it always trades in the direction the price is about to move. So every time it trades with the market maker, the market maker has just sold too cheaply or bought too dearly. That loss is the ochre line, and it is called adverse selection.

Turn the slider up and the ochre line falls faster while the teal line barely changes. The market maker earns the same from uninformed traders whatever you do; what varies is how much it gives away. Somewhere past a few checks a second, the spread stops covering the losses.

The three strategies

Fixed quotes always centres on the current price. It has no way to get rid of a position, so its inventory drifts and its final profit varies wildly from episode to episode. Watch how spread out its circles are.

Leans against inventory shifts both quotes down when it is long and up when it is short, so it becomes a keener seller exactly when it holds too much. Its inventory stays near zero.

Avellaneda–Stoikov is the standard textbook model of optimal market making. It derives both the shift and the width of its quotes from risk aversion, volatility and time remaining. In this market it performs about as well as the hand-tuned version, not better, because its derivation assumes there is no informed trading at all.

How it's built

This page runs an event-driven C++ simulator compiled to WebAssembly, so the model in your browser is the same code as the experiments. If a browser can't run WebAssembly it falls back to an equivalent JavaScript port, and says so under the episode counter. The model: a limit order book with price-time priority, Poisson order arrivals, and a fair value that follows a random walk. Each episode is 300 simulated seconds. The market maker's fills are valued against fair value at the moment they happen, which is how its profit is split between the two groups of traders. The full model, experiments and results are on GitHub.
